# Reframing — from prediction to rationality of response

---

## What the abstract already says

The abstract is essentially correct and does not need to be rewritten.
It already contains the core reframing implicitly. The fiscal deficit
finding is presented first among the substantive results. The threat
effect is described as conditionally significant — only activating after
major geopolitical ruptures. The spatial lag is shown to reflect inertia
rather than strategic behaviour. These are all rationality-of-response
findings, not prediction findings. The reframing is therefore a matter
of emphasis and language, not a change of content.

---

## The shift in emphasis

The current framing leads with threat proximity as the central variable
and presents fiscal constraints as a robust control that happens to
dominate. The reframed version leads with the question of how governments
actually behave — do they respond rationally to threat, and what stops
them when they do not — and presents the findings as answers to that
question.

The findings are identical. The lens changes slightly.

**Current emphasis:** threat proximity predicts defence spending; fiscal
constraints also matter.

**Reframed emphasis:** fiscal constraints are the primary and universal
driver of defence spending; threat proximity matters but its effect is
conditional on whether governments have the fiscal capacity and political
will to respond.

This is a small shift but it changes what counts as the headline result.
The fiscal dominance finding moves from a supporting result to the central
finding. The conditional threat activation in the post-2022 period moves
from a qualification to the empirical puzzle the paper is organised around.

---

## What the reframing changes in the introduction

One sentence in the research question needs a light adjustment. Instead
of asking what drives defence spending, the introduction frames the
question as: how consistently and rationally do governments respond to
external threat, and what is the dominant constraint on that response?

This does not require rewriting the motivation section. The heterogeneity
puzzle described in the abstract — why do countries facing similar threats
spend such different amounts — is already the right entry point. The
reframing simply makes explicit that this heterogeneity is evidence of
constrained or inconsistent response, not just unexplained variation.

---

## What the reframing changes in the results

### Fiscal dominance

Currently presented as: fiscal deficit is a robust negative predictor
across all specifications.

Reframed as: governments facing fiscal pressure do not increase defence
spending even when threat increases. The budget constraint binds
regardless of the security environment. This is the clearest evidence
that defence spending decisions in normal times are driven by what
governments can afford, not by what the security situation demands.

The numbers and models do not change. The sentence that introduces the
finding changes from a statement about correlation to a statement about
behaviour.

### Threat activation only post-2022

Currently presented as: the threat-spending nexus is historically
conditional, activating only after major geopolitical ruptures.

Reframed as: for most of the study period, governments did not translate
nearby conflict into defence spending increases. The post-2022 result is
the exception, not the rule, and it required an extreme and unambiguous
shock to override the fiscal and institutional inertia that normally
dominates. The pre-2022 null is not a model failure — it is direct
evidence that rational threat-responsive behaviour was absent in normal
times.

Again the numbers do not change. What changes is whether the null
result is presented apologetically or as the substantive finding it is.

### Spatial inertia

Currently presented as: the spatial lag reflects spending persistence
rather than genuine strategic diffusion.

Reframed as: countries do not adjust their spending in real time in
response to their neighbours. Each country is locked into its own
historical spending trajectory. This is a third form of the same finding
— alongside fiscal constraint and conditional threat response, spending
inertia confirms that defence budgets are not actively managed in
response to the security environment on any short time horizon.

---

## What does not change

The abstract can remain as written. The models, variables, and all
numerical results are unchanged. The four-regime structure stays. The
threat index construction stays. The spatial framework stays.

The reframing is confined to:
- One sentence in the research question
- The order of emphasis in the results narrative
- The interpretation sentences that follow each set of coefficient results
- The conclusion, which now summarises the findings as evidence about
  the rationality and constraints of government defence spending decisions
  rather than as a predictive model of spending levels

---

## The one-sentence version of the reframed thesis

Governments in NATO-EU member states respond to fiscal constraints
consistently and to external threat only conditionally — spending
decisions in normal times reflect what governments can afford far
more reliably than what the security environment demands, with genuine
threat-driven response emerging only after the extreme and unambiguous
shock of the 2022 Ukraine invasion.
