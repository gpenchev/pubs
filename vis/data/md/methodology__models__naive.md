# Plain Language Summary

## What did this study ask?

Do European NATO governments spend more on defence when they are closer to actual armed conflict? And does it matter whether the government is left or right, pro-EU or Eurosceptic, or whether public finances are tight?

These questions sit at the heart of NATO's burden-sharing debate: are member states rational actors that calibrate defence budgets to threat, or are they free-riders that rely on larger allies regardless of their own exposure?

## What data was used?

The study assembled a 29-year panel (1995–2023) of 22 NATO-EU member states. Defence spending data came from the World Bank and SIPRI. For the threat measure, the study used the UCDP Georeferenced Event Dataset — a record of every armed conflict event (location, date, fatalities) — and built a country-specific annual index that captures how close and how deadly nearby conflicts were. A conflict in Ukraine in 2022 counted much more for Poland or Romania than for Portugal. A conflict in North Africa counted zero, because it was sea-separated.

Fiscal data came from the IMF, economic data from Eurostat, and government political data (left–right orientation, pro/anti-EU stance, election years) from ParlGov.

## What did we find?

**Threat drives spending — but the relationship is not linear across time.**

On average, a meaningful increase in nearby conflict is associated with a roughly 0.09 percentage-point increase in defence spending as a share of GDP. That sounds small, but across a 10-unit increase in the threat index (corresponding roughly to the difference between a quiet year and the Balkans wars period for a neighbouring country), the estimated effect is economically meaningful.

**Fiscal constraints suppressed spending during 2005–2013 even when threat remained elevated.** EU austerity rules and sovereign debt crises effectively prevented governments from responding to threat signals during this decade. The threat–spending relationship essentially disappears in that period and only re-emerges after the 2014 Crimea annexation.

**After 2014, who governs matters more than ever — but not in the way you might expect.** The relevant political division is not left vs. right (that variable is insignificant). It is pro-EU vs. Eurosceptic. Before 2014, pro-EU governments spent marginally more on defence as part of a multilateral commitment posture. After 2014, the pattern reversed completely: Eurosceptic nationalist governments (in Poland, Hungary, and the Baltic states) became the primary drivers of rearmament. Pro-EU governments were more constrained by EU fiscal rules and less inclined to frame defence as a national priority.

**Neighbours matter — but not quite in the way the burden-sharing literature suggests.** Countries with high-spending neighbours do spend more themselves in the long run (spatial lag ρ = +0.177). But this appears to reflect a stable equilibrium in spending *levels*, not short-run imitation: when we remove long-run trends, the relationship actually reverses — a country that increases spending in a given year tends to have neighbours that increase spending *less*, consistent with short-run free-riding.

## What are the limitations?

**The threat measure misses hybrid warfare.** The Crimea annexation in 2014 produced almost no UCDP-recorded fatalities near EU borders, yet it triggered the most significant shift in European defence policy since the Cold War. The study's threat index — built on actual combat deaths — therefore shows almost no signal at the 2014 event, while the GPR (newspaper-based geopolitical risk index) spikes sharply. The UCDP measure performs better over the full sample (lower AIC), but it systematically underestimates threat perception in periods of coercive non-kinetic activity.

**Greece is a persistent puzzle.** Greece spends far above the sample average on defence (mean 2.4% of GDP vs 1.6% for the rest), yet its UCDP land threat score is close to the sample average. This is because Greek threat perceptions centre on the Aegean Sea — a sea-separated theatre that is excluded from the primary land-contiguous measure by design. Greece's high spending is captured by a large positive country fixed effect (the highest in the sample at +1.69), not by the threat coefficient.

**The 2022 Ukraine invasion cannot be studied properly yet.** The 2022–2023 period has only two years of data. Year fixed effects absorb any universal shock that affects all countries simultaneously, so the universal surge in European defence spending after February 2022 disappears into the year dummy. Within-year cross-sections confirm the threat gradient is real in 2022 and 2023, but the panel model cannot yet identify the post-invasion effect.

## Bottom line

European NATO governments do respond rationally to proximate military threat — but the response is conditioned by fiscal rules, political ideology, and the nature of the threat itself. Kinetic, land-accessible conflict drives spending; coercive non-kinetic pressure does not register in the same way. After 2014, the fiscal and political architecture of the EU has become as important a determinant of burden-sharing as geography.
